# Chapter 16 — Mathematical tools: finite probability, entropy, KL

:::tip[TL;DR]
**In short.** The losses of this course are built from three quantities: expectation, entropy, KL divergence. We define them exactly (over the rationals), prove the facts everyone quotes — entropy of a certain event is $0$, cross-entropy $=$ entropy $+$ KL, and the **Gibbs inequality** $\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)\ge 0$ — and run the same formulas on `Float` to see where exactness ends.

**New words, in simple terms:**

- **Distribution**: A list of non-negative weights that add up to 1 — the odds of each outcome.
- **Expectation**: The weighted average of a quantity under a distribution: "on average, what do I get?"
- **Variance**: The average squared distance from the mean: how spread out a quantity is.
- **Entropy**: The average surprise of a draw from a distribution. Certain outcomes surprise nobody: entropy 0.
- **Cross-entropy**: The average surprise of the *true* distribution when scored with a *model* distribution. Training minimises it.
- **KL divergence**: The extra surprise from using the model's code instead of the true one. Always $\ge 0$; $0$ exactly when the two agree.
- **Gibbs inequality**: $\mathrm{KL}(p\|q) \ge 0$: no model ever predicts the data as well as the data predicts itself.
:::


Chapters 3, 6, 9 and beyond all minimise a **cross-entropy** loss, chapters 11 and 13 measure distances between distributions with **KL**, chapter 15 penalises collapsed embeddings with a **variance**. Each time we quoted a property — "cross-entropy is at least the entropy", "KL is non-negative" — without proving it. This chapter pays those debts: it is the toolbox the rest of the course silently assumes.

The chapter is also a methodological turning point. From here, every scalar in a *theorem* is a **`Rat`** — an exact rational, the model in which this course proves (as Chapter 2's perceptron algebra already does). Distributions are finite, on `n` points: enough for every loss in the course, and small enough that "sum" needs no analysis — no limits, no integrals, no epsilon-delta. What the theorems say is exactly true *in this model*; the `Float` experiments satisfy it only up to rounding, and the demo prints the gap.

## Distributions, exactly

```lean file=Core/Prob.lean#dist
```

A `Dist n` is a weight vector `w : Vec Rat n` with two fields: every weight is positive (`pos`), and the weights sum to 1 (`sum1`, written as a dot product with the all-ones vector). The field `n` is a *type-level* number: a distribution on 4 outcomes is a different type from one on 5 — a size mismatch is a type error, not a runtime crash.

The logarithm is the chapter's one piece of analysis, and we do not pretend to construct it: `LawfulLog` is an **abstract operation with named laws** (the same style as `LawfulExp` in the algebra core):

```lean file=Core/Prob.lean#haslog
```

Four laws: multiplication becomes addition, inverses become negation, and the tangent inequality $\log x \le x - 1$, equality only at $1$. Every theorem in the chapter holds for *any* structure obeying these laws. The real logarithm obeys them — that is a theorem of analysis, outside this course — and `Float` obeys them only up to rounding, which is exactly why the statements are over `Rat`.

## Entropy, cross-entropy, KL

Three quantities from one pattern (weighted sum of logs):

- $H(p) = \sum_i -p_i \log p_i$ — entropy: average surprise of `p`.
- $\mathrm{CE}(p, q) = \sum_i -p_i \log q_i$ — cross-entropy: surprise of the data `p` scored with the model `q`. **This is what training minimises.**
- $\mathrm{KL}(p \| q) = \sum_i p_i \log (p_i / q_i)$ — the *extra* surprise of the model's code.

The first identity ties them:

```lean file=Core/Prob.lean#ce_decomp
```

**Cross-entropy = entropy + KL.** The entropy of the data does not depend on the model, so minimising cross-entropy is *exactly* minimising KL: fitting the model, not the data. This is why cross-entropy is the classification loss, not a decorative choice.

## Gibbs inequality: KL ≥ 0

The theorem that makes training mean something:

```lean file=Core/Prob.lean#gibbs
```

The proof is the textbook one, fully checked. The pointwise bound $\log q - \log p \le (q-p)/p$ is the tangent inequality $\log x \le x-1$ applied to $x = q/p$ and rewritten with `log_div`; summing against weights $p_i \ge 0$ preserves the inequality (`dot_mono`); the right side **telescopes** — $\sum_i p_i (q_i - p_i)/p_i = \sum_i q_i - \sum_i p_i = 1 - 1 = 0$ — using the exact cancellation $p \cdot p^{-1} = 1$ that holds over `Rat` and *fails* over `Float`; negating flips the sign. Four steps, each one line of Lean.

**Consequences:** KL is $0$ exactly when $p = q$ (the tangent inequality is strict away from $1$); cross-entropy is bounded below by $H(p)$, so there is a floor no model can beat; and a model that puts near-zero probability on an event that *does* happen pays near-infinite loss — the "confidently wrong" row in the demo.

## Generalization: bias, variance, and why ensembles work

Why does a model that fits the training set perfectly sometimes fail on new data? The answer has an exact form — and here it is a theorem, not a plot. Draw a hypothesis from a family (the randomness of training: data order, seeds, minibatches), score each at every input against the target, average. The identity:

$$\mathbb{E}_{k}\[(f_{k}(x)-t(x))^{2}\] = (\bar f(x)-t(x))^{2} + \mathbb{E}_{k}\[(f_{k}(x)-\bar f(x))^{2}\], \qquad \bar f(x) = \mathbb{E}_{k}\[f_{k}(x)\]$$

**risk = squared bias of the average + variance of the family.** No probability theory beyond two weighted sums, no limits, no asymptotics — it is the algebra of averages, valid point by point:

```lean file=Core/Prob.lean#generalization
```

The engine is one line of reasoning: expand $(f_k - t)^2 = (f_k - \bar f + \bar f - t)^2$, and the cross term $2(\bar f - t)\,\mathbb{E}[f_k - \bar f]$ dies because **the average deviation from the average is zero** (`wavg_dev_zero`). Summing the pointwise identity over the input distribution gives `ensemble_risk_decomp`.

What it buys, exactly:

- **Averaging hypotheses fights variance.** The risk of a single draw is the risk of the average *plus* the variance — the average is never worse. Dropout, bagging, model soups and checkpoints-averaging all cash this check; the theorem is why they work.
- **Only the average can fight bias.** Averaging three underfitting models gives an underfitting average: bias is the gap $\bar f - t$, and no amount of averaging noise closes it. Underfitting is a capacity problem, not a luck problem.
- **Variance is a property of the family, bias of the mean.** High-variance training (small data, deep overparameterized nets, aggressive learning rates) inflates the second term; regularizers mostly trade variance for bias — the decomposition is the ledger.

The demo's last block runs the identity numerically: three noisy models around a line, and `risk − (bias² + variance)` prints `0.000000` — the exact identity surviving rounding.

```
CE(p,q3)   = 2.678650   KL = 1.826791   H+KL = 2.678650   gap = -0.000000

E[p ]·r = 0.740000   Var[p ]·r = 0.242400
E[q1]·r = 0.625000   Var[q1]·r = 0.346875
E[q2]·r = 0.000000   Var[q2]·r = 0.625000
E[q3]·r = -0.625000   Var[q3]·r = 0.346875

Ensemble of 3 models, uniform hypothesis weights:
  total risk        = 0.017500
  (bias of mean)^2  = 0.000278
  variance          = 0.017222
  bias^2 + variance = 0.017500   gap = -0.000000
```

What to read: the good model `q1` costs $0.027$ nats of KL — little extra surprise. The uniform model pays $\log 4 - H(p)$: knowing nothing costs you exactly the missing entropy. The confidently wrong `q3` pays $1.83$: near-infinite when the true event gets near-zero weight. The `gap` column is the honest part: the identity $\mathrm{CE} = H + \mathrm{KL}$ is a **theorem over `Rat`**; over `Float` it holds up to rounding, and the printed gap (here $\sim 10^{-7}$) is the size of that rounding. Expectations and variances of a reward vector show the same formulas doing RL's job (Chapter 13): the model you average over changes the variance of your estimates.

:::warning What is proved, and what is not
Proved, over `Rat` with an abstract logarithm: $E$, variance, $H$, CE, KL satisfy the four expectation laws, the centered-variance identity, $\mathrm{CE} = H + \mathrm{KL}$, Gibbs $\mathrm{KL} \ge 0$, $H = 0$ on a point mass, zero variance of constants, and the bias–variance decomposition of the ensemble risk (pointwise and summed over inputs). **Not proved:** that the real logarithm satisfies `LawfulLog` **Not proved:** that the real logarithm satisfies `LawfulLog` (analysis, outside the course), that the `Float` programs satisfy anything (they round), and that the printed numbers repeat on other distributions beyond rounding. Between the theorem and the experiment sits an explicit, unclosed gap — that gap is the subject of numerical analysis, not of this course.
:::

## Limits and variants

- **Finite is a real restriction.** Continuous densities (the Gaussian KL of the VAE, Chapter 11; entropy of continuous policies) need integrals; nothing in this chapter extends to them without analysis. The formulas carry over by replacing sums with integrals — the proofs do not.
- **The logarithm is assumed, not built.** `LawfulLog` isolates exactly which properties of $\log$ the chapter uses; constructing $\log$ (series, integrals) and proving the four laws is analysis.
- **No base change.** Everything is "natural units" (nats): $\log$ base $e$. Bits (base 2) just rescale $H$ and KL by a constant; the proofs are base-free.
- **`Float` is the wild west.** Every law used here fails for `Float` at the last bit: $(p \cdot q)/p \ne q$ in general. This is why the theorems are over `Rat` and the demo prints the gap instead of hiding it.
